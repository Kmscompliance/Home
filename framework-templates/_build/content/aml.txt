# 3. Our commitment and culture
We will not knowingly deal with criminals or with money that may be the proceeds of crime. We are committed to a culture in which staff feel able to question anything unusual and to raise concerns without fear. Our senior management sets that tone by their own conduct, by making sure financial crime controls are properly resourced, and by acting on what monitoring and reporting tell them.
We apply a **risk-based approach**: we identify and assess the financial crime risks our business faces, and put controls in place that are proportionate to those risks and to the size and nature of our firm.

# 5. Business-wide risk assessment
We maintain a written Business-Wide Risk Assessment (BWRA) that identifies and assesses the risks of money laundering, terrorist financing and other financial crime to which our business is exposed. Firms within the MLRs must do this under regulation 18; firms outside the MLRs do it because it is the foundation of the risk-based controls SYSC 6.1.1R requires.
The BWRA considers:
- **customers** – for example consumers or businesses, customers in vulnerable circumstances, politically exposed persons;
- **products and services** – including the size and frequency of transactions and whether they allow value to be moved quickly or anonymously;
- **delivery channels** – face-to-face, remote/online, or through introducers, brokers or appointed representatives;
- **geography** – where our customers, counterparties and funds come from, including links to high-risk jurisdictions;
- **transactions** – for example cash, third-party payments, overpayments and early settlements;
- the UK **National Risk Assessment** of money laundering and terrorist financing, FCA publications and relevant JMLSG sector guidance.
The BWRA is reviewed at least annually and whenever there is a material change (for example a new product, channel or customer type). Its conclusions decide where we apply enhanced checks, how we target monitoring, and what our training covers. Our BWRA is recorded in the AML Business-Wide Risk Assessment and was last approved by senior management on [Insert date].

# 6. Customer due diligence
Our detailed requirements are in our **Customer Due Diligence Policy**. In summary:
- We identify every customer and verify their identity using reliable, independent sources before we establish a relationship or carry out a transaction, and we understand the purpose and intended nature of the relationship.
- For business customers we also identify and verify beneficial owners and understand the ownership and control structure.
- We apply **enhanced due diligence** where the risk is higher – including, for firms within the MLRs, where the customer is a politically exposed person (or a family member or known close associate), where a transaction is unusually complex or unusually large, where there is no apparent economic or legal purpose, and where a customer or transaction involves a jurisdiction the MLRs identify as high-risk. Since 30 June 2026, the mandatory high-risk third country requirement applies to jurisdictions subject to a FATF “call for action”; other jurisdictions under increased FATF monitoring are assessed on a risk basis.
- For domestic politically exposed persons, the starting point is that they present a lower level of risk than foreign politically exposed persons, unless other risk factors are present.
- We do not proceed with a relationship or transaction if we cannot complete the required due diligence, and we consider whether to make an internal suspicion report.
- We keep customer information up to date through ongoing monitoring.

OPT: 8A | Firms within the MLRs | your conclusion in section 2 is that your firm is within the Money Laundering Regulations (for example consumer credit lenders, payment services firms, investment firms, life insurance intermediaries, and peer-to-peer platforms applying the MLR standard).
**8A.1 Our legal duty.** Because we are within the MLRs, we are in the “regulated sector”. Any member of staff who knows or suspects, or has reasonable grounds to know or suspect, that another person is engaged in money laundering or terrorist financing, based on information obtained in the course of our business, **must** report it to our nominated officer (the MLRO) as soon as practicable. Failing to report is a criminal offence (section 330 of POCA; section 21A of the Terrorism Act 2000). The “reasonable grounds” test means a report can be required even where a person did not actually suspect, if a reasonable person in their position would have done.
**8A.2 How staff report.** Staff report by completing an Internal Suspicious Activity Report (ISAR) and giving it directly and confidentially to the MLRO [Insert how ISARs are submitted, e.g. by secure email]. Staff must not discuss the suspicion with the customer or anyone else, and must not carry out further investigation themselves. Once a report is made to the MLRO, the member of staff has met their legal obligation.
**8A.3 What the MLRO does.** The MLRO acknowledges the report, reviews it against the information we hold, records their reasoning in the AML Incident Register, and decides whether there is knowledge or suspicion requiring a report to the NCA. If so, the MLRO submits a Suspicious Activity Report (SAR) through the NCA’s online reporting portal as soon as practicable. The MLRO’s decision is theirs alone and must not be influenced by commercial considerations.
**8A.4 Defence against money laundering (DAML).** If we need to carry out an act that would otherwise be a money laundering offence (for example processing a payment or returning funds), the MLRO will request a DAML from the NCA and we will not proceed until consent is received or deemed. The NCA has seven working days to respond; if it refuses, a further moratorium period applies (normally 31 days, which a court can extend).
**8A.5 Tipping off.** It is a criminal offence for anyone in the regulated sector to disclose that a SAR has been made, or that an investigation is under way or contemplated, where that disclosure is likely to prejudice an investigation (section 333A of POCA; section 21D of the Terrorism Act 2000). Staff must handle customers carefully while a report is under consideration and must not give any indication of it. The MLRO will advise on what to say.
**8A.6 Records.** The MLRO keeps a confidential record in the AML Incident Register of every ISAR, the MLRO’s decision and reasons, any SAR submitted (with its NCA reference) and any DAML correspondence, separate from customer files.

**8.1 Signs of possible financial crime.** Staff must be alert to anything unusual. Common warning signs include:
- reluctance or refusal to provide identification or information, or information that cannot be verified;
- information that is inconsistent (for example income that does not match employment, or an address the customer does not seem to know);
- payments from, or refunds requested to, third parties or accounts in a different name;
- unusually large, complex or rushed transactions without an obvious purpose, or deliberate splitting of payments to stay below thresholds;
- overpayments followed by refund requests, or early settlement soon after taking out a product;
- the customer appearing to be directed or controlled by someone else;
- links to high-risk jurisdictions, or requests to route money through unusual channels.
Every warning sign is judged in context. One sign does not automatically mean a report is needed, but staff should ask themselves whether a reasonable person would be suspicious – and if in doubt, report.

# 10. Record keeping
We keep records that allow us to demonstrate compliance and to respond to law enforcement and the FCA. We keep:
- customer due diligence records and supporting evidence – for **five years** after the end of the business relationship or the date of an occasional transaction (regulation 40 of the MLRs, for firms within the MLRs; firm policy for others);
- records of transactions sufficient to reconstruct them – for five years after the transaction;
- internal suspicion reports, MLRO decisions, SARs and DAML correspondence – for [Insert period, e.g. five years] after the report;
- sanctions screening results and decisions;
- training records and the business-wide risk assessment, including previous versions.
We delete personal data at the end of the retention period unless we are required by law, or it is necessary for legal proceedings, to keep it longer, in line with our Data Processing Policy.

# 11. Training and awareness
Every member of staff receives financial crime training appropriate to their role:
- at induction, before they deal with customers or payments;
- at least annually after that; and
- whenever the law, our risks or our procedures change materially.
Training covers: the law and our obligations; how money laundering, terrorist financing, sanctions evasion and fraud might occur in our business; warning signs; how to report suspicions; tipping off; data protection in relation to financial crime; and our internal procedures.
